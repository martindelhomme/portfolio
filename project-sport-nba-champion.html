NBA Analytics - Predictive Model - Backtesting - 10 seasons

Can Regular Season Statistics
Predict the NBA Champion?

Using the efficiency metrics from Articles "Has the Three-Point Revolution Changed What Drives Winning?" and "What Statistics Best Predict Winning in Today's NBA?" to build a simple champion score, then backtesting it against 10 seasons of results.

Seasons tested
2015-16 to 2025-26
Champion 2025-26
New York Knicks
Tools
Python, D3.js
Method
Composite Score + Backtest
01 - The Question

If we only knew the regular season stats...

Articles "Has the Three-Point Revolution Changed What Drives Winning?" and "What Statistics Best Predict Winning in Today's NBA?" established that Net Rating and shooting efficiency are the dominant predictors of regular-season winning. But does that translate to playoff success? If you built a model based purely on regular-season data, could you have identified the eventual champion?

This is the analyst's version of a fantasy question. The goal is not to claim we can predict the champion with certainty, the NBA playoffs are a small sample, subject to injuries, matchups, and randomness. The goal is more modest: does the model consistently identify a small group of realistic contenders that includes the eventual winner?

The analogy to finance is direct: this is a backtest. We build a model on historical data, apply it to each season, and measure how well it would have performed out-of-sample.

"The question is not 'does the model predict the champion?', it's 'does the model consistently put the champion among the top contenders?' That's a much more honest and useful benchmark."
02 - The Model

A composite score built on Article "What Statistics Best Predict Winning in Today's NBA?" findings

From this article, the variables that best explain regular-season winning are Net Rating (r = 0.965), eFG% (r = 0.564), Defensive Rating (r = -0.512), and TOV% (r = -0.368). The model weights these four factors, with Net Rating carrying the most weight given its dominant explanatory power.

To make scores comparable across seasons, each variable is z-scored within the season before weighting, this removes the effect of year-to-year shifts in league averages (e.g. pace changes, scoring inflation).

Champion Score Formula
Score = 0.40 × z(Net Rating)
        + 0.30 × z(eFG%)
        + 0.20 × z(–Def Rating)
        + 0.10 × z(–TOV%)

All variables are z-scored within each season. Defensive Rating and TOV% are inverted (lower is better). The weights reflect the correlation strength found in Article "What Statistics Best Predict Winning in Today's NBA?", Net Rating dominates, followed by shooting efficiency, then defense, then turnovers.

from scipy import stats
import pandas as pd
import numpy as np

# Champions by season
champions = {
    2015: 'Warriors', 2016: 'Warriors', 2017: 'Warriors',
    2018: 'Raptors', 2019: 'Lakers', 2020: 'Bucks',
    2022: 'Warriors', 2023: 'Celtics',
    2024: 'Thunder', 2025: 'Knicks'
}

# Z-score within season, build composite score
def season_zscore(df, col):
    return df.groupby('season')[col].transform(
        lambda x: (x - x.mean()) / x.std())

agg['z_net'] = season_zscore(agg, 'net_rtg')
agg['z_efg'] = season_zscore(agg, 'efg')
agg['z_def'] = -season_zscore(agg, 'def_rtg')
agg['z_tov'] = -season_zscore(agg, 'tov_pct')

agg['champion_score'] = (
    0.40 * agg['z_net'] + 0.30 * agg['z_efg'] +
    0.20 * agg['z_def'] + 0.10 * agg['z_tov'])

# Rank teams within each season
agg['model_rank'] = agg.groupby('season')['champion_score'].rank(ascending=False)

# Backtest: what rank was the eventual champion?
results = []
for season, champ in champions.items():
    season_df = agg[agg['season'] == season].sort_values('champion_score', ascending=False)
    champ_row = season_df[season_df['teamName'] == champ]
    top3 = season_df.head(3)['teamName'].tolist()
    results.append({
        'season': f"{season}-{str(season+1)[-2:]}",
        'champion': champ,
        'model_rank': int(champ_row['model_rank'].values[0]),
        'top3': top3
    })
    print(f"{results[-1]['season']}: {champ} ranked #{results[-1]['model_rank']} | Top3: {top3}")

05 - 2025-26 Season

The Knicks: ranked #6 by the model

The 2025-26 NBA Champion is the New York Knicks. The model ranked them #6 in the league based on regular-season stats, with the Thunder(lost in WCF), Spurs (Runner-up), and Nuggets holding the top spots by efficiency metrics.

This is the model's biggest miss of the decade, but it also illustrates the core limitation honestly: regular-season efficiency predicts regular-season success. The playoffs are a different competition. The Knicks' championship is a testament to exactly what the model can't capture: playoff execution, matchup advantages, coaching adjustments, and the health and form of key players in June.

The Knicks were a legitimate contender by the model, top 6 in the league. But the gap between #1 and #6 is exactly where playoff basketball lives: the randomness of a 7-game series, the impact of a single injury, and the ability to execute under pressure are not in the data.

06 - Limits

What this model cannot do

Small sample size. 10 seasons is not enough to draw robust statistical conclusions about a predictive model. The results are directionally interesting, not statistically definitive.

Regular season vs playoffs. The model is trained on regular-season efficiency. Playoff basketball involves opponent-specific preparation, series adjustments, and home court effects that don't appear in 82-game averages.

Dynasty effects. Teams like the Warriors (2022-23, ranked #9) deliberately manage regular-season effort to preserve players for the playoffs. A model based on regular-season stats will systematically underrate teams with this strategy.

Injuries and health. The model has no injury data. A top-ranked team that loses a key player in Round 1 will show no sign of weakness in the regular-season stats used to build the score.

Weight sensitivity. The 40/30/20/10 weights were chosen based on correlation strength, not optimized through cross-validation. Different weight combinations would produce different results, and the model has not been tested for robustness to weight changes.

07 - Conclusion
A reliable contender filter — not a crystal ball
  • The model correctly identifies the champion within its top 5 in 7 of 10 seasons. That's a meaningful result, it's much better than random, but well short of a reliable prediction.
  • The model is best understood as a contender filter: it systematically eliminates the bottom 25 teams and identifies a group of 4-6 realistic championship candidates from regular-season data alone.
  • Net Rating and eFG% do most of the work. The model's misses (2022-23 Warriors, 2025-26 Knicks) are not random errors, they reflect specific structural limitations: dynasty coasting and playoff-specific performance differences.
  • The Knicks' 2025-26 championship is the model's most instructive miss. Ranked #6 by efficiency, they won it all, a reminder that the playoffs reward execution, not regular-season dominance.
  • This three-article series mirrors a quantitative investment framework: identify the relevant factors (Article 1), measure their explanatory power (Article 2), build and test a predictive model (Article 3). The NBA's data richness makes it a uniquely accessible laboratory for this kind of structured analysis.
